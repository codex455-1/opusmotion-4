# Research & fact sheet — *The Mitrokhin Archive*

Every line of narration and every on-screen label was checked against published, credible sources.
Where sources disagree, the film uses the wording that all of them support.

## Narration (as recorded)

> In 1992, an old man walked into a British embassy… with a scruffy bag of sausages.
> Under the meat, were the secrets of the KGB.
> Vasili Mitrokhin. A KGB archivist.
> In 1972, he was put in charge of moving the KGB's spy archive, to a new headquarters outside Moscow.
> Every day, he read the files. And copied tiny notes by hand… hidden in his shoes.
> At his country house, he buried them in milk churns, under the floor.
> Twelve years. Nobody caught him.
> When the Soviet Union fell, he tried the Americans first. They didn't take him seriously.
> The British gave him a cup of tea.
> Then MI6 smuggled him and his family out of Russia. The notes followed.
> Inside were hundreds of names. One of them lived in a quiet London suburb.
> Melita Norwood. A grandmother, unmasked at eighty-seven… who had passed Britain's atomic bomb secrets to Moscow.

## Deliberately avoided (unsupported or contested)

- Naming Riga or Vilnius as *the* British embassy city. Sources conflict, so the film says "the Baltics".
- "MI6 officers disguised as workmen dug up six trunks." Accounts conflict; Corera says Mitrokhin carried most of it out himself. The film says only "The notes followed."
- "The KGB never found out", and "200,000 documents / 25,000 pages". Neither is supported.
- "She gave the Soviets the bomb." The film says she passed *secrets* of Britain's atomic bomb project.
- Real likenesses of Mitrokhin or Norwood. The portrait is an anonymous halftone, and every scene is labelled an illustrative reconstruction.
